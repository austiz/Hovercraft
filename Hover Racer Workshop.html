Hover Racer Workshop

How does it float?

Have you played air hockey? The table blows air up, and the puck rides on a thin pillow of air, so it slides with almost no rubbing. Your hovercraft is an upside-down air hockey table: it carries its own fan, blows air down, and rides on its own pillow of air.

Because nothing rubs on the ground, even a whisper of air out the back is enough to move it. And in this event, slow is exactly what you want.

How you win (the official scoring)

At the start of your turn, the judges announce a Target Time between 6 and 18 seconds. Every team gets the same one. Your job is to cross the 185 cm timed stretch in exactly that time while carrying as many nickels as you can. You get two runs, your best run counts, and it's out of 100 points:

40Finish points: reach the finish line without stopping
40Time points: full points at the exact Target Time, fewer the further off you are
20Nickel points: carry all the judges' nickels (about 1 kg) for all 20
  • Keep moving! If the craft sits still for 3 seconds, the run ends where it stopped.
  • Your timing stick must cross the start line within 3 seconds of launch, or that run doesn't count.
  • Finish within 2× the Target Time, or the run is incomplete.
  • Nickels that fall off don't count, and falling parts cost a penalty. Build good trays (Step 6).
  • The judges offer a launch ramp. Don't use it. It halves your finish points and wipes out your nickel points.
Win

Finishing is worth the most, so the #1 rule is never stall. The #2 rule is to be exact. A craft that crawls at a speed you can pick and repeat wins this event.

Grown-up

Before you order: keep the motor exactly as listed: 12 V and 22,000 RPM. A motor built for a different voltage can look identical but spins too slowly or burns out. Avoid the popular “RS-550S 18 V” version; on 12 V it only reaches about 13,000 RPM, which is too weak. When parts arrive: slide the adapter onto the motor shaft (3.17 mm) and press on a propeller, using the rings that come with the props if the hole is loose. Check the motor's two screw holes too (usually M3 screws, 25 mm apart). Do both before you print the fan tube and motor mount.

Rule

Only simple electric parts are allowed: batteries, wire, switches, relays, resistors, capacitors, and up to 2 motors. No computer chips, no lasers. (No chips also means no remote control.)

1

Cut the deck

The deck is the body. Cut the foam board to 36 cm × 34 cm. Then cut two openings:

  • A round hole, 12 cm across, in the middle. The lift fan blows through it, so it's almost as wide as the 5-inch propeller. A smaller hole would choke the fan.
  • A 3 cm × 10 cm slot near the back edge, centered left to right. That's where the nozzle drinks air from the skirt.

Why this size? Bigger decks float more easily. Double the size and floating gets four times easier. So we use the biggest deck that still fits the 40 cm rule box, saving a little room for the puffy skirt.

Grown-up

Use a sharp craft knife on the foam board, and help with the cutting. For the round hole, trace a bowl or print a 12 cm circle template.

2

Make the skirt

The skirt turns a foam board into a hovercraft. It's also the part every team rebuilds a few times, so don't worry if your first one isn't perfect. Take it in four mini-steps.

2a · Cut the plastic

Cut the thick plastic into a rectangle 46 cm × 44 cm, which is 10 cm bigger than the deck each way. The extra plastic lets the skirt puff into a pillow about 3 to 4 cm deep. Too tight and it can't inflate; too baggy and it wobbles and bulges out of the rule box.

2b · Tape it on

Flip the deck upside down. Tape the sheet around the bottom 1 cm in from the edge, leaving it baggy like a loose pillowcase. (Why 1 cm in? The rule box is measured while your craft is floating, and the puffed skirt bulges out past the deck. Taping inboard keeps it inside 40 cm.) The skirt covers both deck openings from underneath. That's how the fan fills it and the nozzle drinks from it.

  1. Start with clear packing tape in the middle of each side. Smooth every wrinkle before taping over it, because a wrinkle under the tape is a tiny tunnel that leaks air.
  2. Work from the middles toward the corners. At each corner, fold the extra plastic into one flat pleat (like wrapping a present) and tape it down flat.
  3. Run a second lap of duct tape on top of the first, all the way around. The air pushes hard on this seam.

2c · The wear pad and the air holes

Cut a 14 cm circle from a milk jug or foam board and duct-tape it to the outside center of the bag, on the side facing the ground. That's the wear pad, the craft's foot. It takes the rubbing when you set it down, and it keeps the air holes just off the floor so they never get squished shut.

Stick a patch of clear tape where each hole will go, then cut six 2.5 cm holes (about the size of a quarter) through the tape, in an even ring about 3 cm out from the pad's edge. The tape patches stop a hole from tearing into a rip. Start with this size and only make them bigger if the float test says to.

2d · The first float test

Put it on a smooth floor, flip on the lift fan, and watch it like a scientist. Whatever it does, there's a fix:

If it does this…Try this
Leans or tips to one sideThe holes or the weight aren't even. Re-space the holes, or move the battery holders until it sits flat.
Bounces and jiggles like a basketballThe holes are too small and the bag is over-puffed. Make each hole a little bigger.
Barely lifts, drags on the floorThe holes are too big or the craft is too heavy. Make a new skirt with smaller holes, or take weight off.
Plastic gets sucked up toward the fanInstall the guard under the deck hole (Step 3).
A hole tears into a ripPatch it with tape, and next time cut every hole through a tape patch.
Smart

You can always make a hole bigger. You can never make it smaller. Start small and sneak up on it.

Test

Test it again with all the nickels on board (Step 6). It must still float well fully loaded, because that's how you'll race.

3

Mount the lift fan

Put the motor in the 3D-printed tube over the 12 cm hole, with the propeller blowing down into the skirt. Glue the tube's base to the deck all the way around so no air escapes. Turn it on: the skirt should puff up like a marshmallow and the craft should float. If it struggles with the nickels aboard, use fresh batteries or take weight off.

The RS-550 is a strong motor, about 200 g, so print the motor mount solid: 4 walls and 40% infill, held by two M3 screws. It spins the propeller about 17,000 times a minute, so do a spin test before anything else.

  1. With the fan cage on, run the motor fully loaded for one minute.
  2. Switch it off and look at both propeller blades. Any crack, bend or white stress mark means throw that prop away and use a new one.
  3. Check the adapter is still tight on the shaft.
Smart

Stick with the 2-blade 5030 propellers. Tough 3-blade drone props look stronger, but they take about twice the power to spin and would drain the batteries fast.

Rule

“All propellers/impellers, including those under the vehicle, must have shielding.” Print the cage and guard with openings of about 7 mm and test them with a pencil (about 7 mm thick) before the judges test with their 9.5 mm stick. A craft that fails the safety check doesn't get to run.

4

Build the nozzle and speed plates

This is the design's secret weapon. The Target Time is 6 to 18 seconds, which is crawling, slower than walking. So instead of a second fan, we let the squished air in the skirt do the pushing. A 3D-printed nozzle sits over the slot at the back of the deck. It drinks air from the bag and squirts it out straight backward through a round socket.

Into that socket you press a speed plate: a flat printed disc with a hole in it. Big hole = more air = faster. Small hole = slower. Each plate is labeled with the time it makes, so on race day you just pick the plate that matches the Target Time.

What to print

  • The nozzle: an elbow that glues over the 3 × 10 cm slot and turns the air to point straight back, centered left to right. It ends in a round socket about 50 mm across. Glue it down airtight, because a leak around the base is air that doesn't push.
  • Speed plates: flat discs about 3 mm thick that press snugly into the socket, each with a round hole. Print the number big on each one. Start with just three test plates with holes of 15, 22 and 30 mm. Step 8 shows how to work out the rest.
  • A “zero” plate with no hole. You use it for the drift test (Step 8).
  • A plate rack: a little holder with numbered slots, so the plates travel with the craft and never get mixed up.

And here's the magic: load more nickels and the craft gets heavier, so the air in the bag gets squished harder. That pushes air out the nozzle harder, all by itself. The extra push almost exactly makes up for the extra weight, so each plate gives nearly the same time whether the craft is empty or full. The judges will ask how your craft works, and this answer impresses them.

5

Wire it up

With the nozzle doing the pushing, the wiring is one simple loop. The three battery holders connect in a chain (+ of one to − of the next) to make 12 V. Then it goes batteries → switch → motor → back to the batteries.

Grown-up

Help connect the wires with the lever connectors, and check the chain goes + to − between holders. Never let the bare + and − wires touch. Before gluing anything down, test: the motor should spin fast and blow down. If it blows up, swap the two motor wires.

Power

The motor pulls about 4–5 amps while running, and a big burst for an instant when it starts. That's why the switch is rated 30 A and the wires are thick 20 gauge. Push each battery firmly into its holder and keep the springs clean, because a loose battery wastes power. The motor gets warm in long tests, so give it a minute's rest between batches.

Rule

At impound, your craft is turned in with the batteries stored separately, so keep the holders easy to reach, not buried under tape.

6

Print the nickel trays

The judges provide the cargo: 4 rolls of nickels (about 200 g each), 1 half roll, and 20 loose nickels, about 1 kg in all. Carrying all of it earns the full 20 nickel points. The rules say no tape, no glue, and no opening the rolls, so the nickels just sit in whatever your craft offers. Print trays with walls.

  • A nickel roll is about 21 mm across and 80 mm long. Make the slots about 22 mm wide and 84 mm long, and test them with the real rolls you got from the bank.
  • Make the slot walls at least 12 mm tall. A roll that falls off costs points and a penalty.
  • Print a small bin with a lip for the half roll and the loose nickels.
  • Always carry the full load, and load it the same way every time (same rolls in the same slots). Where the weight sits changes how the craft drifts, so the same load every run means the same time every run.
Win

Because of the nozzle's magic (Step 4), carrying all the nickels barely changes your time. So always carry all of them: 20 points, and you only need to practice one way.

7

Add the timing stick

The race is timed by light beams at the start and finish, like the sensor on a garage door. Your craft carries a wooden stick that breaks the beams. The rules are picky, so get this exactly right:

  • A ¼-inch (or thicker) wooden dowel, standing straight up in a printed socket
  • Right at the front edge. The rules allow up to 3 cm back, but on a slow run the craft starts so gently that the stick needs every centimeter to cross the start line within 3 seconds.
  • The top of the stick at least 20 cm above the track while floating. Cut it to about 24 cm to be safe.
  • Nothing on the craft sticks out in front of it
8

Practice: make your speed plates

On competition day you get the Target Time, then 8 minutes and just 2 runs, with no practice runs allowed. So all the learning happens at home. First, build the official track:

  • Two straight 2×4 boards taped to a smooth floor, 45 cm apart and at least 240 cm long
  • A start line at least 45 cm from the start end, and a finish line 185 cm past it
Batteries

The 20-minute rule: one charge gives about 30 minutes of motor time, but the power fades near the end, and that would throw off your plate times. So recharge after about 20 minutes of running, and split the full calibration into two practice sessions. Use the same set of 10 batteries for practice and the race.

8a · The drift test

Press in the zero plate (no hole), load all the nickels, and float the craft in the middle of the track. With no air out the back, it should just sit there. If it slowly creeps one way, something is out of balance: the batteries, the trays, or an uneven skirt. Fix that first. The craft is super sensitive, so a slow run depends on it floating perfectly still.

Smart

If it drifts the same way wherever you put it, the floor might be tilted instead. Turn the craft around. If it drifts toward the same end of the room, it's the floor, so find a flatter spot to practice.

8b · Time the three test plates

Fully loaded, with the craft touching the start line, time each test plate (15, 22 and 30 mm) 3 times. Measure from when the stick crosses the start line to when it crosses the finish line. Write down the average for each plate.

8c · Find your craft's magic number

Here's a fun math fact: for this craft, hole size × time stays about the same. A hole twice as wide makes it twice as fast. So multiply each plate's hole size by its time:

magic number = hole size (mm) × time (seconds)

Say the 22 mm plate took 11 seconds. Then 22 × 11 = 242. Check the other two plates. They should give numbers close to that, so use the average of all three.

8d · Print the full set

Now work out the hole for every Target Time by dividing:

hole size = magic number ÷ Target Time

With a magic number of 242, the full set looks like this (yours will be different):

Plate6789101112131415161718
Hole (mm)40353027242220191716151413

Print all 13, then test every plate 3 times and write the real times on your speed chart. If a plate is off by more than half a second, reprint it a millimeter bigger (faster) or smaller (slower).

Rule

Your speed chart must be impounded with the craft in the morning, because no new notes can come in later. Make two copies and impound both. The plate rack goes in too, as spare parts.

States

At State and National tournaments the Target Times come in half seconds (like 12.5). Just print half-second plates too, using the same division.

Pass inspection: the rules checklist

Before you race, a judge checks your craft. Go down this list at home so there are no surprises:

  • The whole craft (stick included) fits in a 40 × 40 × 40 cm box, measured while it's floating, so the puffed-up skirt counts too
  • The fan has a cage on top and a guard below that a 9.5 mm stick can't poke through
  • Store-bought batteries with their original labels easy to see, adding up to 12 volts or less by the labels (we chose rechargeable AAs, but the rules allow other kinds)
  • No lithium or lead batteries, no computer chips, no lasers
  • The motor has a switch you can flip with your fingers (no twisting wires together)
  • It floats, and pops back up when pushed down
  • Both teammates have safety glasses
  • You can explain how you built it, because you built it. Judges ask, and the speed plates are a great story!

Race day, step by step

  1. The night before: charge all 10 batteries. The charger holds 8, so it takes two rounds; start early. Pack safety glasses, the craft, the plate rack, spare props, spare skirt plastic, tape, a calculator, and both copies of the speed chart.
  2. Impound (morning): turn in the craft with the batteries stored separately, plus the plate rack, spare parts and speed chart.
  3. Your turn begins: safety glasses on. The judges announce the Target Time and your 8 minutes start. Put the batteries in.
  4. Pick your run-1 plate from the speed chart. If the Target Time is 14 seconds or more, use one plate faster (for example, plate 15 for a 16-second target). A slow run that stalls loses almost everything, and being a little early only costs a point or two.
  5. Load all the nickels the usual way. Dry-sweep the track if it's dusty (that's allowed).
  6. Place the craft with its front touching the start line. The judge holds a wood block in front of it.
  7. Flip the switch and let the skirt puff up. Hands off, then count down out loud: “3, 2, 1, launch!” The judge pulls the block away.
  8. Don't touch it, not even a nudge, until the judge says you may. Touching it means the run scores zero.
  9. Run 2: fix it with math. Use the calculator:
    new plate = plate you used × Target Time ÷ your run-1 time
    For example, with a Target Time of 12, plate 12 ran 13.0 seconds: 12 × 12 ÷ 13 = 11.1, so use plate 11. This fixes whatever that track's floor does. If run 1 stalled, use two plates faster.
Win

Everything you decide on race day comes from a card you made at home and one division problem. No guessing, no nerves. That's engineering!
